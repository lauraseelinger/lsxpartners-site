I'm getting my house ready to sell — and my husband and I are putting everything in storage because we're about to travel for a year or two. So for the last few weeks I've been running the entire project through AI to help with my Type-A timeline planning.

And I mean the *entire* project. I don't think I opened Google once, except maybe to find where to actually buy something AI recommended.

First I needed faucets, so I described the bathrooms and asked for midrange options that'd look good without costing much (I'm selling, not renovating for the next owner). Then carpet — where cleaning fit in the timeline before showings, and which companies actually handle pet stains, because, well, three cats. Then packing — I asked about bins for long-term storage, and AI recommended Sterilite gasket bins. I found the size I wanted at Costco, grabbed the rest at Walmart, done.

Not once did I type a keyword. Not once did I "search." I described my situation, one problem at a time, and followed the thread.

A storage brand got recommended to me and bought by me. The only time I touched Google was to find where to buy it, and I never landed on the brand's own website at all. That's the whole reason "AI visibility consultant" and "SEO agency" are two different jobs.

**The short version:** An SEO agency optimizes your website to rank for keywords so someone clicks through. An AI visibility consultant makes sure your brand shows up — and gets cited — when your buyer describes their problem to AI and never types a keyword at all. Same foundation, different game, and you probably need both working together.

## Is AI visibility just SEO with a new name?

This is the objection I hear most, and I get why. So let me be fair to it: they're related. You can't be cited by AI if your site is a technical disaster that can't be crawled or ranked — the foundation is shared. (If you want the deeper version of what "getting cited by AI" even means, that's my [answer engine optimization guide](/blog/answer-engine-optimization-guide).)

But the goal is where they split. SEO is about ranking a page so a human clicks a blue link. AI visibility is about being the answer — getting quoted, cited, or recommended inside the response, often when there's no click happening at all.

So it's not a rebrand. It's the same starting point pointed at a different finish line.

## What are you actually optimizing for?

Keywords versus prompts — and honestly, versus whole conversations.

An SEO agency optimizes for keywords: short, clipped phrases. The average Google search is only a few words. Five years ago my faucet hunt would've been "bathroom faucet matte black," click, compare, done.

That's not what happened. I never typed a keyword. I described my situation — selling, not renovating, here's the bathroom — and then kept going, one problem to the next, across faucets and carpet and packing. AI visibility optimizes for *that*: the messy, specific, full-sentence way people describe what they're actually navigating. And increasingly it isn't even one prompt — it's a running conversation where AI recommends something (hi, Sterilite) and the person goes and buys it.

So the work isn't "what keywords do we rank for." It's "what is my buyer actually saying when they describe the problem my product solves — and are we the answer AI gives back?" I figure that out through audience intelligence — going where your audience actually talks and pulling out the real patterns and vocabulary. (I wrote about how [here](/blog/audience-intelligence/).) You can't optimize for a conversation you've never heard your buyer have.

## Where does the SEO agency stop and the consultant start?

Roughly: the SEO agency's world is your website. The AI visibility consultant's world is the whole internet — because that's where AI reads.

Your website should be the foundation. Get it optimized, get it clean, check that box. But AI builds its answer from everywhere — Reddit, YouTube, LinkedIn, industry publications, review sites. The brands that get cited aren't the ones with the best homepage. They're the ones showing up, clearly and consistently, across all the places AI actually pulls from.

So a big part of my job that an SEO agency usually doesn't touch: figuring out how you become part of those third-party conversations instead of letting the entire internet write your story for you.

## Can't my SEO agency just add an "AI visibility" report?

A lot of them are, and here's where I'll push back — because I've watched those add-on scores mislead people.

I had a client whose SEO tool showed an AI visibility score of 85% — "leading across platforms." Looked fantastic. You'd screenshot that into a deck and feel great. But when I clicked into the individual platforms, a competitor was clearly ahead on Perplexity, and that "leading" headline read the same on every tab, including the one we were losing on.

Then I ran the actual questions AI asks about the problem that brand exists to solve. They had 0% share of voice on 94% of them. Both numbers were technically true. They were measuring completely different things — one measured aggregate positioning, the other measured whether the brand showed up when a real buyer asked a real question. (I dug into why single AI scores mislead [over here](/blog/what-your-ai-visibility-score-cant-tell-you).)

That's the gap. An add-on gives you a number. A strategist tells you whether the number means anything, and what to do next — which is the part no dashboard does for you.

## What does an AI visibility consultant actually do, then?

Fair question — "AI visibility" can sound vague. For me it's an ongoing loop, not a thing you do once and walk away from. It runs in a few stages:

- **An audit first** — what AI actually says about your brand right now, across ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, and Google's AI, and where the gaps are. You can't fix what you haven't measured, and most brands are guessing. (Here's [how I run one from scratch](/blog/how-to-audit-ai-visibility).)
- **Audience intelligence** — the real questions and the real words your buyers use, so we optimize for what they actually ask instead of a list your team brainstormed in a meeting.
- **Strategy on your own site** — what changes, what gets added, what questions each page needs to answer so AI can pull a clean answer straight from you.
- **The third-party layer** — Reddit, YouTube, LinkedIn, industry press. AI trusts those more than your homepage, so a big piece of the job is getting you into those conversations instead of letting everyone else write your story for you.
- **Ongoing monitoring** — your competitors move and the models change, so what got you cited in June might not in September. I run a monthly check on where we gained, where we slipped, and what to pull next. (I call the whole feedback loop [the AI Intelligence Loop](/blog/ai-intelligence-loop).)

An SEO agency usually isn't set up for most of that — their world is your website ranking, and this reaches well past it.

## So which one do I actually need?

Honestly? Usually both, doing different jobs.

Keep the SEO work — the foundation is real and AI often pulls from what already ranks. But if your buyers are describing their problems to AI and getting your competitors back in the answer, an SEO report won't even tell you it's happening. That's the layer a consultant owns: the ongoing measurement, the audience intelligence, the audit that catches where you're invisible, and the strategy to fix it for *your* specific brand in *your* specific industry.

If you've got an SEO agency you trust, great — keep them. Bring in someone like me for the part they can't see. That's not me being diplomatic; it's just where the line actually is.

## Do you always need to hire this out?

Not necessarily. If you're a small brand with a little time to experiment, you can absolutely start on your own — ask AI about your own category, watch which sources it keeps citing, and go make yourself present in those places. You'll learn a lot just doing that.

Where a strategist makes the difference — and honestly where a small business gets the most out of one — is the interpretation. I've spent over 16 years in marketing strategy, so when I look at a pile of AI outputs I'm reading more than where you show up. I'm reading *why* you do or don't, what your buyer is actually thinking when they ask the question, and which single change will move the most. That's the psychology underneath the data, and it's the part someone newer to marketing wouldn't catch yet. A small business can punch way above its weight with that kind of read — you skip months of trial-and-error and go straight to the levers that matter.
